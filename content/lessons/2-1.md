## Brief

A prompt is a brief. Nothing more mysterious than that. The reason "prompt engineering" became a thing is that most people write briefs the way they'd text a friend, and then wonder why the output is generic.

Four parts move the quality of an answer more than anything else:

- **Goal.** What you want, as an outcome. Not "help me with my email" but "write a reply that gets them to confirm the date."
- **Context.** What Claude can't know: who you are, who it's for, what's happened, why it matters, constraints. The thread. The numbers. The backstory.
- **Format.** What shape the answer should take. Two paragraphs. A table. Bullet points, no more than five. A subject line and body. Plain language, no jargon.
- **Example.** What "good" looks like. A past email you were proud of. A paragraph in the voice you want. One example beats three paragraphs of description.

You don't need all four every time. A quick question is a quick question. But when the output matters, the missing part is almost always the reason the output is bad.

A test you can run on any prompt before you send it: **could a smart stranger act on this?** If you handed your message to a capable person who'd never met you, could they produce what you want? If not, Claude can't either.

## Task

The bad prompt rewrite. You're going to feel the difference, not read about it.

1. **Run the bad prompt.** Open a new chat and paste this exactly:
```
Write an email to my client about the delay.
```
   Read what comes back. Notice how generic it is. It had no choice.
2. **Write the better prompt.** In a new chat (new, not the same one), write a prompt for the same outcome using all four parts: goal, context, format, example. Invent a realistic situation if you don't have one. Something like: who the client is, what the project is, what the delay is and why, what you want them to do next, how you normally sound. Paste a real email of yours as the example if you have one.
3. **Compare the two outputs side by side.** Open both chats. Read them back to back.
4. **Score it.** Ask yourself: which one could I actually send? Which one sounds like me? What single piece of context made the biggest difference?
5. **Write that answer in your notes below.** One sentence. That's the lesson, in your words.

> If your better prompt still produced something you wouldn't send, that's useful. Figure out which of the four parts was thin, add it, and run it again.

## Takeaways

- The four parts: goal, context, format, example. When output is bad, one of these is missing.
- Test before sending: could a smart stranger act on this message? If not, neither can Claude.
- One real example of "good" beats paragraphs of description.
